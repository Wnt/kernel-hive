## Origins

Commodore introduced the Amiga 500 in 1987 as the lower-cost, home-oriented member of the Amiga family, priced at $699 in the United States. Its keyboard, processor, disk drive, and expansion connections occupied a single wedge-shaped case, but the architecture descended from the ambitious Amiga 1000. A Motorola 68000 CPU ran at roughly 7 MHz alongside a set of custom chips. Agnus managed memory access and supplied the blitter and Copper coprocessor; Denise generated graphics; Paula handled sampled sound, disk access, and input/output. Dividing the work this way let the machine animate, scroll, and play four-channel audio while the central processor continued with other tasks.

Workbench was the graphical face of AmigaOS. Version 1.3, paired here with Kickstart 1.3, presents disks, drawers, tools, and a menu bar on the characteristic blue desktop. Beneath it, AmigaDOS supplied a command line and filesystem, while the Exec kernel scheduled tasks pre-emptively. In a late-1980s home computer, color graphics, sampled stereo sound, a mouse-driven desktop, and responsive multitasking formed an unusually coherent whole.

## Significance

The Amiga’s importance comes from the way specialized hardware and operating-system design met creative practice. Deluxe Paint made the machine a fixture in pixel art and television graphics. Trackers, samplers, and Paula’s four channels of 8-bit sample playback at 32 kilohertz supported a distinctive music culture. Games used the blitter, hardware sprites, and smooth scrolling to produce motion that contemporary general-purpose PCs struggled to match without costly add-on hardware.

Workbench also revealed that the Amiga was more than a games console with a keyboard. Applications could remain active behind one another; screens of different resolutions could be pulled down like layers; command-line and graphical tools shared the same machine. Its rough edges—limited memory protection, floppy dependence, and hardware-specific conventions—belong to an era when a personal computer’s identity was inseparable from its chipset.

## What you're looking at

The original A500 shipped with 512 KB of chip RAM, commonly expanded through the trapdoor slot.

## Legacy

Its 8-bit forebears are also in this museum: the VIC-20 and the Commodore 64 ran one-megahertz 6502-family CPUs, and the Amiga’s 7 MHz 68000 was the same company’s leap to a 16-bit future. The Amiga 500 became especially influential in Europe, where it bridged home computing, games, demoscene production, and semi-professional creative work. Its custom-chip strategy eventually became difficult to advance against rapidly improving commodity graphics and sound hardware, and Commodore’s 1994 collapse ended the platform’s mass-market trajectory. Yet the machine’s effects survive in pixel-art practice, tracker music, game development, and a preservation community that treats hardware timing as part of the work itself. Workbench 1.3 captures the platform before later refinements: spare, direct, and unmistakably designed around what the Amiga could do in parallel.
