## Origins

**General Magic** was founded in 1990 by **Marc Porat**, **Bill Atkinson** and
**Andy Hertzfeld**, who took the "Paradigm" project out of Apple to build it as
a company of its own. Atkinson had written MacPaint and QuickDraw, Hertzfeld
much of the original Macintosh system software, and Porat had written the memo
that argued the next personal computer would be a pocket communicator rather
than a desk machine. Their answer had two halves: **Magic Cap**, an operating
system for a small touchscreen device with a stylus, and **Telescript**, a
language for software agents that could travel across a network, run on someone
else's machine, and come back with an answer.

The first products shipped in 1994 — the **Sony Magic Link PIC-1000** and the
**Motorola Envoy** — talking to **AT&T PersonaLink**, a network built to carry
Telescript agents. They arrived into a world of 9,600-baud modems and no public
internet access to speak of, and they did not sell. AT&T closed PersonaLink in
1996. General Magic also shipped Magic Cap as a **Windows-hosted simulator** —
the same Magic Cap code, running on a period PC instead of a handheld
processor. This exhibit runs a **1995 pre-release build (327)** of that
simulator, the earliest build found sourceable for the museum.

## Significance

Magic Cap is the most complete attempt anyone has made to build a personal
computer with no windows and no files. There is no desktop metaphor in the
Macintosh sense, no folder hierarchy, no application menu bar. Instead there is
a **place**: you start at a drawn **desk**, with a telephone, a notebook, a
datebook, and an **in box** and **out box** sitting on it. Touching the phone
gives you the phone. Touching the notebook gives you the notebook. Along the
bottom edge are **stamps** — you post a message by putting a stamp on it — and
**drawers** holding tools and stationery, which slide open over whatever you are
doing.

Behind the desk is a **hallway**, reached through the doorway at the back of the
room. The spatial metaphor is carried all the way down; the system has no other
way of organising itself, and nothing is hidden behind a name you have to
remember.

Underneath it, Telescript was doing something the industry took another twenty
years to want: sending a small program to where the data was, instead of
dragging the data to the program.

## What you're looking at

A **1995 pre-release build (327)** of General Magic's Windows-hosted Magic Cap
simulator, running on a period PC. The screen is the handheld's — a small
touchscreen you tapped with a stylus — and the pointer on this exhibit takes the
stylus's place: this is a **pointer-first** system, and the keyboard is only for
typing text into something you have already opened.

Start at the desk. The objects on it are the programs, so touching one opens it;
the drawers and stamps along the bottom edge slide out when touched. The doorway
at the back of the room leads to the **Hallway**; navigation beyond that is not
part of what this exhibit has proven, so the desk and the hallway are the two
places to expect.

This Magic Cap has no network. PersonaLink was gone by 1996, and the exhibit
does not pretend a service is still answering: the phone dials, the out box
fills, and nothing leaves. The machine resets to the desk between visits, so
anything you write belongs to your visit.

## Legacy

General Magic failed as a company and became one of the most consequential
failures in the industry. **Tony Fadell** went on to lead the iPod and then the
iPhone; **Andy Rubin** went on to create Android; **Pierre Omidyar** went on to
found eBay. The pocket communicator Porat described in 1990 — always with you,
always connected, and the thing you use instead of a computer — is the device
most people now carry, roughly fifteen years later than he wanted it. The 2018
documentary **General Magic** is the company telling that story in its own
words.

What did not survive is this screen. Every touchscreen device since went back to
a grid of application icons; nobody shipped the hallway again. This pre-release
build of Magic Cap is an early look at the one system that thought a computer
should be a place.
