## Origins

Miles Gordon Technology was a Swansea company that made accessories for somebody else's computer. Its DISCiPLE and +D interfaces gave a ZX Spectrum a disk drive and a printer port and a snapshot button, and they sold well enough that Alan Miles and Bruce Gordon — both ex-Sinclair Research — decided to build the machine those add-ons had been compensating for. The SAM Coupé went on sale at the end of 1989, six years after the Spectrum it was aimed at and two years after Sinclair had sold the name to Amstrad.

The hardware reads as a Spectrum with the limits taken off. A Zilog Z80B runs at 6 MHz rather than 3.5. Memory starts at 256 KB, twice the largest Spectrum ever shipped, and goes to 512 KB internally — which is what this station has, because it is what the emulator fits as standard. A single custom ASIC, designed in-house, does the video, the memory paging and the I/O that a Spectrum needed a board full of discrete logic and a lot of contended-memory compromise to achieve. The screen modes go up to 512 by 192 pixels, or 256 by 192 in sixteen colours chosen from a palette of 128 — a genuine palette, reprogrammable a line at a time, on a machine whose ancestor could show two colours per character square. A Philips SAA1099 gives six channels of stereo sound. One or two 3.5-inch drives fit in bays in the case, so this is a British 8-bit that assumed an 800 KB floppy where its ancestor had assumed a cassette.

It could also, in software, pretend to be a 48K Spectrum, which mattered commercially and turned out to matter less than MGT hoped: the compatibility was good but not complete, and by 1989 the people who wanted to run Spectrum games mostly already owned a Spectrum.

## The software people actually ran

SAM BASIC is the first thing the machine does and the reason it is on this station's menu. It was written by Dr Andy Wright, whose Beta BASIC had been the Spectrum's serious BASIC extension, and it carries that lineage: named procedures with parameters, DO/LOOP, WHILE, a full-screen line editor, structured graphics commands, and none of the Spectrum's one-key keyword entry — on a SAM you type the words. It is a language somebody could actually write a program in, which is not something that could be said of every 1980s ROM BASIC.

SAMDOS is the disk operating system on this station's boot floppy, and it does one thing that makes the exhibit possible: on boot it automatically loads and runs the first file on the disk whose name begins with `auto`. That file here is a short SAM BASIC program that prints the menu and waits for a key — the same trick every SAM disk of the period used to start itself.

Behind the numbers are four programs from the machine's own commercial catalogue, all from 1992, by which time the SAM had a small and stubborn software scene rather than a market. Revelation Software converted **Manic Miner** and published **Mr. Pac**; the Manic Miner is Matthew Smith's 1983 caverns redrawn for a machine with colour to spare. **Splat!** was an Incentive Software maze game, converted for the SAM by Colin Jordan with digitised speech by Edwin Blink — the kind of credit list that only appears when the conversion is a labour of interest. **The Secretary**, by A. N. Stevens, is the application: a word processor with user-definable graphics, printer control codes and its own disk commands, sold to the people who had bought a SAM to work on rather than to play on.

## Legacy

MGT went into receivership in 1990, less than a year after the launch, undone partly by a fire at its floppy-drive supplier that stopped it delivering machines into its first Christmas. Miles and Gordon bought the assets back as SAM Computers Ltd and kept going until 1992; West Coast Computers picked up the design after that and made machines in small numbers for years. Somewhere around twelve thousand SAM Coupés were built in total, against the millions of Spectrums it was meant to succeed.

That is the whole story of the machine, and it is not the whole story of the platform. The commercial titles on this disk were all written after the manufacturer had already failed once, for an audience of a few thousand people, and the SAM scene went on producing demos, games and hardware into the 2000s. The SAM Coupé is the clearest case in this collection of a computer that was too late to win and too good to abandon.
