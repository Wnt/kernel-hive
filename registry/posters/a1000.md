## Origins

The Amiga 1000 was announced in July 1985 at the Lincoln Center in New York, and it was not really Commodore's machine. It was designed by a small California company, Amiga Corporation, around a set of custom chips that Jay Miner's team had begun as a games console; Commodore bought the company in 1984 and shipped the result as a computer. A Motorola 68000 ran at 7.16 megahertz beside those chips — Agnus for memory access, the blitter and the Copper display coprocessor, Denise for graphics, Paula for four channels of sampled sound and for the floppy drive. The processor could hand the hard work to them and carry on. In 1985, when a personal computer meant one program at a time in one colour scheme, this one multitasked pre-emptively, in colour, with stereo sound and a mouse.

The launch machine had 256 KB of memory and a front expansion slot for 256 KB more, which is where nearly all of them ended up. Its most unusual decision was where the operating system lived. On the A1000 the Kickstart — the part of AmigaOS that on later Amigas sat in read-only memory — arrived on a floppy disk. The machine booted a small bootstrap chip, asked for the Kickstart disk, loaded a quarter of a megabyte of operating system into a special write-protected area of memory, and only then asked for Workbench. Commodore did it because the software was not finished when the hardware was; the side effect was a computer whose operating system could be replaced by handing the owner a new disk.

## Significance

The A1000 is the machine the whole Amiga line grew out of. The A500 that filled European bedrooms two years later and the A2000 that went into television studios are the same architecture in cheaper and larger boxes. What the custom chips made possible — smooth scrolling, hardware sprites, four-channel sample playback, genlocking video to a broadcast signal — set the terms for a decade of games, demoscene work, pixel art and desktop video, and the habits formed here outlasted the hardware by a long way.

It is also the most personal-feeling computer Commodore ever shipped. The inside of the top cover is moulded with the signatures of the engineers who designed it, Jay Miner's dog Mitchy included — a gesture borrowed from the Macintosh team, and the sort of thing that survives in a machine's reputation long after its specifications stop mattering. Fewer than a hundred thousand were sold. Almost everything the Amiga is remembered for started in this case.

## What you're looking at

An emulated Amiga 1000 — 68000, the original OCS chipset, 512 KB of chip memory — running Kickstart 1.2 and booting Workbench 1.2 from a floppy, which is how an A1000 owner started every session. Double-click a disk icon to open its window; the drawers, the menu bar you pull down from the top, and the plain blue backdrop are the desktop as it stood before Workbench 1.3 tidied it. This exhibit takes the operating system from a Kickstart image the emulator holds in place of the bootstrap chip; the machine's own two-disk startup, Kickstart first and Workbench second, is a route the lab is still working on.

## Legacy

The floppy-based Kickstart lasted exactly one model. From the A500 onwards it was a chip on the board, and the A1000's disk drive went back to being just a disk drive. The architecture ran on until Commodore's collapse in 1994, and the operating system outlived the company: the museum's Amiga 500 exhibit runs Workbench 1.3, and AmigaOS 3.5 — released in 1999 by a German developer for machines whose maker had been gone for five years — is here too. This exhibit runs on FS-UAE, part of the family descended from UAE, the emulator begun in 1995 as the "Unusable Amiga Emulator" and named before anyone found out how good it would get.
