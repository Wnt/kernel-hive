## Origins

Android began at Android Inc. in 2003 and was acquired by Google in 2005. The first commercial handset, the HTC Dream, appeared in 2008 with a pop-up slide-out QWERTY keyboard, 192 megabytes of RAM, and 256 megabytes of storage, and a Linux-kernel-based operating system organized around applications, touch interaction, telephony, and cloud services. Google and partners published much of the platform as the Android Open Source Project, while proprietary services and device-specific components formed a separate commercial layer.

The architecture did not simply place a desktop Linux distribution on a phone. Android defined its own application framework, lifecycle, permission model, user interface toolkit, and managed runtime—first Dalvik and later ART. Hardware abstraction and a common software target allowed manufacturers to build many different devices, though vendor customization and delayed updates also fragmented the ecosystem.

## Significance

Android made the smartphone a mass global computing platform. Its availability to numerous manufacturers supported devices across wide price ranges, and its application model turned phones into cameras, maps, media players, payment devices, messengers, and general network clients. The system shifted personal computing toward sensors, continuous connectivity, and software distributed through centralized stores.

Its openness is layered and sometimes contested. AOSP supplies inspectable foundations, yet ordinary devices depend heavily on proprietary drivers, services, and certification. The platform’s scale has also made its choices about permissions, background activity, updates, and data collection consequential well beyond traditional operating-system design.

## Legacy

Android became the most widely deployed family of general-purpose operating systems by device count. It carried Linux into pockets at a scale desktop distributions never reached and established application, gesture, and notification conventions now taken for granted. Its legacy includes both extraordinary hardware diversity and the enduring problem of maintaining software across long, vendor-dependent device chains — and even that problem has a sequel in this museum, where the postmarketOS station shows community-built Linux moving into the handsets their makers abandoned and keeping them in service.
