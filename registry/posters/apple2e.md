## Origins

The Apple II of 1977 was Steve Wozniak's machine: a 6502 chosen because it cost twenty-five dollars, a 14 MHz crystal that set both the colour signal and the 40-by-24 text screen, a display circuit that refreshed the memory chips as a side effect of drawing the picture, and a BASIC written without an assembler in less than four kilobytes. Its address map ran out at 48 KB, where the I/O space began, and a whole industry of "language cards" grew up to squeeze 16 KB more into the gap. By 1980 that 64 KB machine with a Disk II drive was the standard, and every commercial program shipped on a floppy.

The Apple //e of January 1983 was the rebuilt version of that design: a full keyboard with lower case, four cursor keys, a Delete key, and two new modifiers, Open-Apple and Closed-Apple, that were wired to the joystick buttons rather than the keyboard. Its auxiliary slot took an 80-column card carrying another 64 KB, which is how a //e came to have 128 KB and, less obviously, the double high-resolution graphics mode that the extra memory bank made possible. The enhanced //e of 1985, which this station emulates, swapped in a 65C02 and new ROMs; the platinum model of 1987 added a numeric keypad and shipped with the 128 KB configuration as standard.

## The software people actually ran

ProDOS, Apple's second disk operating system for the II, arrived in late 1983 and is what a business or school machine booted. On the boot volume, a BASIC program named STARTUP runs automatically, the Apple II's answer to AUTOEXEC.BAT, and that is exactly what this station uses: a 40-column menu that launches each program with a single keypress and comes back when the station is reset.

AppleWorks, written by Rupert Lissner and published by Apple in 1984, was the program the //e was bought for. It joined a word processor, a spreadsheet and a database under one keystroke-driven interface, used the 80-column screen properly, and by 1985 was reported as the best-selling software title on any computer in the United States. Claris took it over in 1987, and version 3.0 followed in 1989. It is keyboard-only by design: the Open-Apple key plus a letter does everything, and the Delete key works the way a modern visitor expects, which it does not at the BASIC prompt.

Dazzle Draw, from Brøderbund in 1984, was the other reason to have the 128 KB machine. It painted in double high resolution, 560 by 192 pixels in sixteen colours, drove the Apple mouse card, and put brushes, fills, text and a slideshow on a machine that could otherwise show six colours. It was the closest thing the 8-bit Apple had to MacPaint.

Angry Birds is the odd one out. It was written for the Apple II in 2023 by 8-Bit Shack as a tribute, uses the double low-resolution mode that the 80-column card also unlocked, and needs an enhanced //e. It stands here as proof that the 1983 hardware still has people writing for it, and because the machine it runs on is the same one the two older programs were written for.

## Legacy

Apple kept building the //e until 1993, and the //e card for the Macintosh LC until 1995, close to nineteen years of one architecture. Schools bought it by the district, at prices that by 1987 undercut the Commodore competition, and then kept the machines for a decade because they still did what they were bought for. What the //e settled was the shape of the personal computer as an appliance for text: crisp 80 columns, a fast disk and enough memory for a real word processor mattered more to a business than colour and sound, and on that ground the Apple II held its own against machines with far better graphics. The GEOS desktop in the next exhibit shows what the same hardware could be persuaded to do in 1988; this station shows what it was doing all along.
