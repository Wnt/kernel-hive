## Origins

The Lisa was announced in January 1983 at $9,995, the first computer sold to the public with a mouse, a bitmapped screen and a desktop of overlapping windows. Apple had started it in 1978 as a conventional business machine and rebuilt it around what a delegation, including Steve Jobs, had seen at Xerox PARC in December 1979: windows, icons, a pointing device, and the idea that a document on the screen should behave like a document on a desk. Over four years a team of around ninety people turned that visit into a complete office system — an operating system with protected memory and preemptive multitasking on a Motorola 68000, a graphics library, and seven integrated applications written to one interface standard.

The machine on this screen is the Lisa 2 of 1984, the revision that replaced the original's two unreliable "Twiggy" floppy drives with a single Sony 3.5-inch drive and dropped the price. Lisa Office System 3.1, also 1984, is the last release Apple made before the product was folded into the Macintosh line as the Macintosh XL and then discontinued in 1985.

## Significance

The Lisa is where most of the desktop you use today was first put in front of a paying customer. Pull-down menus, the menu bar across the top of the screen, double-clicking to open, dragging to move and to throw away, dialog boxes, the clipboard with cut, copy and paste between programs — all of it shipped here a year before the Macintosh, and in a more thorough form. The Macintosh took this design and made it fit a $2,495 machine with 128 KB of memory; the Lisa had a megabyte and used it.

What the Macintosh left behind is the part worth looking at. The Lisa Office System is **document-centric**: you do not start LisaWrite and then open a file, you tear a fresh sheet off a *stationery pad* on the desk, and the tool comes with it. Documents keep their windows, their positions and their contents across a power cycle, because the system saves the whole desktop when you turn the machine off and restores it when you turn it back on. There is no "save" in the sense a Macintosh user learned; the document on the screen is the document on the disk. Thirty years later, iOS reinvented much of this.

## What you're looking at

An emulated **Apple Lisa 2** — Motorola 68000 at 5 MHz, 1 MB of RAM, the revision H boot ROM of February 1984, and a 5 MB ProFile hard disk on the parallel port — booting **Lisa Office System 3.1** from a disk with all seven Lisa tools installed: LisaWrite, LisaDraw, LisaCalc, LisaGraph, LisaList, LisaProject and LisaTerminal, plus the Clock and Calculator.

The screen is the Lisa's own 720 by 364 pixels, drawn with the tall pixels the real monitor had, so proportions are right. At rest you see the desktop as the system boots it: the menu bar, the Preferences, Wastebasket and Clipboard icons, and the ProFile icon labelled "Disk" at the bottom right. Double-click the disk and the folders for each tool open; inside each is the tool and its stationery pad. Double-click a pad to tear off a sheet, then double-click the sheet to open it. Turning the machine off and on again — the reset button here — brings back the disk as it was shipped.

This Lisa has no network. In 1984 it had a pair of serial ports and an AppleNet card that never quite arrived, and the exhibit does not pretend otherwise.

## Legacy

Apple sold perhaps a hundred thousand Lisas and buried the unsold ones in a Utah landfill in 1989. The ideas did better. The Macintosh team took the interface almost whole; the Lisa's own operating system, with its memory protection and multitasking, was what the Macintosh would not have again until Mac OS X in 2001. In 2023 the Computer History Museum released the Lisa's source code, and the emulator this exhibit runs on, LisaEm, has kept the machine bootable since 2007. The Office System on this screen is the version of the graphical desktop that lost, and the one that had the most in it.
