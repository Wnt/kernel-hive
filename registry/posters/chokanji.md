## Origins

In 1984 Ken Sakamura, at the University of Tokyo, began TRON — The Real-time Operating system Nucleus — an attempt to design a whole computing world from the silicon up: chips, an embedded kernel, a human interface, all specified in the open. BTRON was the desktop branch, the "Business" TRON meant to be the personal computer for everyone. Its two defining ideas were unusually ambitious for the 1980s. First, the interface was built around persistent objects: every document is a "real object" (実身) that lives forever, and windows onto it are "virtual objects" (仮身) you can embed inside one another and link freely — hypertext as the native file system, years before the Web. Second, and the reason for the name 超漢字 ("super kanji"), it carried a character set large enough to write not just modern Japanese but the full historical CJK canon and eventually scripts from around the world — tens of thousands of characters where DOS made do with a few thousand.

Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry backed a plan to put BTRON machines in the nation's schools. Prototypes were built; a generation of Japanese children was to grow up on an operating system Japan had designed for the world.

## Significance

It never happened. In 1989 the Office of the United States Trade Representative, wielding the "Super 301" provision of the 1988 Omnibus Trade Act, listed TRON among potential trade barriers — a signal that adopting it could draw sanctions. Facing that pressure the school-computer plan collapsed, and Japanese classrooms filled with MS-DOS and, later, Windows PCs instead. It is one of the sharpest what-ifs in computing history: a home-grown, openly specified OS with a universal character set, shelved for a rival built abroad.

BTRON did not die. Its embedded sibling ITRON, unencumbered by any desktop ambition, quietly became one of the most widely deployed operating systems ever made, running in billions of appliances, cameras, engine controllers and spacecraft. And the desktop lineage survived commercially: Personal Media Corporation shipped 1B/note, then from 1999 the 超漢字 (Chokanji) line — B-right/V, a BTRON3 system — right up through 超漢字V, keeping Sakamura's real-object desktop and its colossal character set alive for anyone who wanted it.

## What you're looking at

This is B-right/V, the BTRON3 kernel under the 超漢字 desktop, running on emulated PC hardware. The blue wallpaper is tiled from the very kanji the system exists to render. The window at upper left is a real-object box — its rows are live objects, not filenames — and the tall panel is the "原稿箱" of paper types: manuscript paper, drawing paper, a browser, mail, a spreadsheet, cards, script paper, even programming languages, each a document you open rather than an application you launch. The pointer is a pointing hand, the BTRON signature.

## Legacy

TRON is both the great lost desktop and one of the great invisible successes of computing. The interface you are looking at is the road not taken — what a mainstream personal computer might have felt like had it been designed around living, linkable documents and an unbounded writing system rather than files and 8-bit code pages. That the same project, in its embedded form, went on to run more processors than any Western OS only sharpens the point. The museum keeps 超漢字 as the surviving, runnable face of the operating system Japan built for the whole world.
