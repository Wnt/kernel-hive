## Origins

In 1984 Ken Sakamura, at the University of Tokyo, began TRON — The Real-time Operating system Nucleus — an attempt to design a whole computing world from the silicon up: chips, an embedded kernel, a human interface, all specified in the open. BTRON was the desktop branch, the "Business" TRON meant to be the personal computer for everyone. Its two defining ideas were unusually ambitious for the 1980s. First, the interface was built around persistent objects: every document is a "real object" (実身) that lives forever, and windows onto it are "virtual objects" (仮身) you can embed inside one another and link freely — hypertext as the native file system, years before the Web. Second, and the reason for the name 超漢字 ("super kanji"), it carried a character set large enough to write not just modern Japanese but the full historical CJK canon and eventually scripts from around the world — tens of thousands of characters where DOS made do with a few thousand.

Japan's Ministry of Education, together with the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI), drew up a standard for the computers the nation's schools would use — built around BTRON. Prototypes were built; a generation of Japanese children was to grow up on an operating system Japan had designed for the world.

## Significance

It never happened — though not for the single reason the story is usually told with. In April 1989 the Office of the United States Trade Representative named BTRON in its annual report on foreign trade barriers, objecting that an operating system the Japanese government was pushing into Japanese schools, and that ran only in Japan, would lock American firms out. The naming swept TRON into that spring's furore over "Super 301," the aggressive trade provision of the 1988 Omnibus Trade Act — yet TRON was never one of the provision's designated targets (those were supercomputers, satellites and forest products), and it was dropped from the reckoning after US officials visited the TRON Association that May. The pressure, formal or not, was enough: by June 1989 a Japanese government unwilling to strain the relationship set the school plan aside, and Japanese classrooms filled with MS-DOS and, later, Windows PCs instead.

The trade fight was not the whole story. BTRON was already behind — its hardware late, its many industry partners hard to align — and might have faltered on its own merits. But the episode did a subtler, lasting damage: electronics makers, wary of provoking the United States, kept their distance from BTRON for years afterward. It remains one of the sharpest what-ifs in computing — a home-grown, openly specified OS with a near-universal writing system, pushed to the margins at the moment it might have gone mainstream.

BTRON did not die. Its embedded sibling ITRON, unburdened by any desktop ambition, quietly became one of the most widely deployed operating systems ever made — running in countless appliances, cameras and engine controllers, and in both of Japan's Hayabusa asteroid probes. And the desktop lineage survived commercially: Personal Media Corporation shipped 1B/note, then from 1999 the 超漢字 (Chokanji) line — B-right/V, a BTRON3 system — on up through 超漢字V, keeping Sakamura's real-object desktop and its colossal character set alive for anyone who wanted it.

## What you're looking at

This is B-right/V, the BTRON3 kernel under the 超漢字 desktop, running on emulated PC hardware. The blue wallpaper is tiled from the very kanji the system exists to render. The window at upper left is a real-object box — its rows are live objects, not filenames — and the tall panel is the 原紙箱, the box of blank stationery — one master sheet for each kind of document: manuscript paper, drawing paper, a spreadsheet, cards, mail, a web browser, script paper, even programming languages. Each is a TAD document you open, not an application you launch — the very distinction BTRON set out to erase. The pointer is a pointing hand, the BTRON signature.

The edition running here is B-right/V 4.202, from the 超漢字4 era. Personal Media's later consumer release, 超漢字V, ships as a Windows-hosted virtual machine rather than a bootable disk of its own — but that is a difference of packaging, not of system: the BTRON3 desktop and the giant character set on show are the same lineage.

## Legacy

TRON is both the great lost desktop and one of the great invisible successes of computing. The interface you are looking at is the road not taken — what a mainstream personal computer might have felt like had it been designed around living, linkable documents and an unbounded writing system rather than files and 8-bit code pages. That the same project, in its embedded form, became one of the most widely deployed operating systems on Earth only sharpens the point. The museum keeps 超漢字 as the surviving, runnable face of the operating system Japan built for the whole world.
