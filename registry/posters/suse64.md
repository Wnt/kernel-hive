## Origins

SuSE began in Nuremberg in 1992 as a four-person software and consulting company, and its first Linux was a German-language edition of Slackware on floppies. By the end of the decade it had grown into Europe's largest Linux vendor, and the product it sold was as much a book as a disc: a green cardboard box holding six CDs, a 444-page printed manual, and a period of installation support by telephone. SuSE Linux 6.4 shipped in March 2000, the last of the 6.x line, and the name still carried its small u — the company was "Gesellschaft für Software- und System-Entwicklung", and the capitalisation was a joke it had not yet grown out of.

Under the box art was kernel 2.2.14, XFree86 3.3.6 with the new 4.0 series supplied alongside for the adventurous, KDE 1.1.2 as the default desktop with GNOME 1.0.x installed beside it, StarOffice 5.1, and Netscape Communicator 4.7. Holding it all together was YaST — "Yet another Setup Tool" — SuSE's own installer and administration program, in its text-mode form and, new in this generation, a graphical front end that ran the first install. YaST was what made a SuSE box different from a Red Hat one: a single tool that partitioned, chose packages, wrote the X configuration and then stayed on the system to manage it afterwards.

## Significance

6.4 sits at the exact point where desktop Linux stopped being a hobby and started being a product. KDE 1 gave a Unix workstation a panel, a file manager that doubled as a web browser, and a consistent widget set; StarOffice gave it a word processor a business could accept; and SuSE's manual meant a newcomer could get from a blank disk to that desktop without a Usenet account. The six-CD format itself is a document of its moment — more software than a dial-up line could carry, so it came by post.

It is also a snapshot of a transition in the machinery underneath. XFree86 3.3.6 still needed its display server chosen per graphics card; 4.0, on the same discs, introduced the single modular server that every later X used. Kernel 2.2 was the last generation before the 2.4 line brought USB and large-memory support into the mainstream. Within a year SuSE would drop the small u, and within four it would belong to Novell.

## What you're looking at

The station boots a snapshot of a KDE 1.1.2 desktop at 1024x768 in sixteen-bit colour, with kpanel along the bottom and a konsole window open. The pointer is absolute — it goes where you put it — and the keyboard is an ordinary PC keyboard, so the konsole is yours to type into. `cat /etc/SuSE-release` tells you which SuSE you are on, and `/usr/X11R6/bin` is where the X programs of the era still lived.

## Legacy

SuSE Linux became SUSE Linux, then openSUSE and SUSE Linux Enterprise, and YaST survives in both to this day — the longest-lived installer in the Linux world. The green chameleon on the box top is still the company's mark. What did not survive is the object in the box: the printed manual, the telephone support, the sense that an operating system was a thing you bought at a shop and carried home.
