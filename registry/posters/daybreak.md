## Origins

**In April 1981 Xerox shipped the 8010 Information System — the Star — and invented the desktop.** Icons for documents and folders, overlapping windows, WYSIWYG editing, property sheets, a two-button mouse, Ethernet, and a laser printer at the end of the wire: the whole vocabulary, in one product, a full three years before the Macintosh. It cost about $16,500 for one workstation and rather more for the file and print servers it needed to be useful, and it sold in the low tens of thousands.

The 6085 is what Xerox did next. Announced in 1985 and built around a new Mesa processor — internally *Daybreak*, sometimes *Dove* — it did the same work as a Star for roughly a third of the money, in a low flat case that fitted under a desk instead of beside it. The software was renamed too: **ViewPoint**, a repackaging of the Star environment where the applications were sold as separately licensed options, so a customer could buy the desktop and the document editor and skip the spreadsheet. It also, unlike the Star, could run MS-DOS on an optional coprocessor board in a window, which tells you exactly what the market had become between 1981 and 1985.

## Significance

The 6085's importance is not that it was new — it is that it was the same. Four years after the Star, and a year after the Macintosh, Xerox was still shipping ideas nobody else had caught up with.

Look at the screen. There is no title bar: a window carries a **named tab** along its top edge, with the commands that apply to it — Close, Redisplay, Start, Cancel — as buttons in the same strip. There are no menus in the modern sense; almost every setting in the system is reached through a **property sheet**, a form you fill in and confirm, and the same idiom covers a paragraph's margins and the machine's own configuration. Across the top of the display runs a **message area** that describes what the system is doing in complete sentences — *Please type your password and then press &lt;NEXT&gt;* — rather than in status codes.

And the keyboard is not a PC keyboard. Down its left side ran a column of dedicated keys for the actions the interface is built from: **NEXT, OPEN, PROPERTIES, MOVE, COPY, SAME, AGAIN, FIND, UNDO, HELP, DELETE**. These were not shortcuts for menu items; there mostly were no menu items. Select a thing, press MOVE, indicate where — the verb is a key on the keyboard. It is a genuinely different theory of how a person and a computer divide the work, and it is the one piece of the Star that did not survive into everything that followed.

## What you're looking at

A Xerox 6085 with the 19-inch large-screen display: 1152 by 861 pixels, black and white, no greys — the soft mid-tone of the desk is a fifty-percent dither, which is why it shimmers slightly. Running on it is ViewPoint 2.0.5, with the basic workstation, the document editor, the spreadsheet, NetCom and the office accessories all enabled.

The desk is nearly empty on purpose. There is no file server and no Clearinghouse on the other end of this machine's Ethernet — the whole XNS office it was designed to live in is gone — so ViewPoint has done what it was built to do when the network is unreachable: it created a **temporary desktop** and carried on. The single icon in the bottom right is the **Directory**, and it is the way in: opening it reaches the workstation's own filing, the workspace, and the network that is not there.

The exhibit answers to the machine's own keys. The on-screen keyboard carries the Level-V function block, and each button sends what this emulator expects: NEXT, OPEN, PROPERTIES, MOVE, COPY, SAME, AGAIN, FIND, UNDO, HELP, STOP, DELETE. Clicks want a deliberate press — this machine was never in a hurry.

## Legacy

Xerox kept selling ViewPoint systems into the 1990s, then moved the environment onto ordinary PCs as GlobalView — the Mesa machine reimplemented in software, running under Windows 3.1, the last commercial form of a lineage that began at PARC in 1973 with the Alto. GlobalView was discontinued in the late 1990s and that was the end of it.

What survives is everything and nothing. The icons, the folders, the overlapping windows, the mouse and the WYSIWYG page are so completely absorbed into ordinary computing that they are invisible. The rest of it — the property sheet as the universal way to change anything, the message area narrating the system in prose, and above all a keyboard whose keys are the verbs of the interface — was not copied, and looking at this screen is the only way left to see what the alternative felt like.
