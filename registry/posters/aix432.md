## Origins

In 1994 IBM, Apple and Motorola were two years into an alliance built on a shared conviction: that the PowerPC processor could displace Intel on the desktop. The processor was the easy part. The hard part was that a chip alone is not a computer, and nobody wanted a repeat of the incompatible workstation architectures of the 1980s. So IBM wrote a specification — the **PowerPC Reference Platform**, PReP — describing a machine assembled from ordinary PC parts, a PCI bus, an ISA bridge, a standard SCSI controller, on which any PowerPC operating system ought to run.

The **RS/6000 Model 40P**, machine type 7020, was IBM's own answer to that specification, announced in 1994 at $3,995. Inside was a 66 MHz PowerPC 601, memory expandable from 16 MB to 192 MB, SCSI-2, two serial ports, a parallel port, and business audio with a speaker — a Unix workstation built, deliberately, out of the same commodity components as the PC on the next desk. Its firmware was IBM's, codenamed Dakota. The nearly identical mainboard was also sold in a different case, with different firmware, as the Power Series 440.

The ambition was genuinely broad. A PReP machine was meant to boot AIX, and also Windows NT, OS/2, Solaris and NetWare — an open PowerPC desktop that any vendor could build and any operating system could target.

## Significance

Almost none of that happened. Windows NT for PowerPC shipped and was quietly abandoned; the PowerPC ports of OS/2 and Solaris barely reached daylight. Within a year PReP was superseded by CHRP, a joint IBM–Apple revision, and the whole idea of a standard PowerPC desktop faded as the alliance's ambitions narrowed. What survived on these machines was the operating system IBM had always intended them to run.

AIX is IBM's Unix, first shipped for the RS/6000 line in 1990 and still under development today — one of the very few commercial Unixes to outlive its era rather than be retired with the hardware. It is also distinctly *itself*: where other Unixes edit text files, AIX keeps its device and software configuration in the ODM, a binary object database, and its own commands are the only supported way in. The version here, 4.3.3 from 1999, is the last of the 4.3 line.

Its desktop is **CDE**, the Common Desktop Environment — the result of a 1993 truce in which HP, IBM, Sun and Novell agreed to stop shipping four incompatible desktops and standardise on one, derived from HP's VUE. For roughly a decade CDE was simply what a commercial Unix workstation looked like, on every vendor's hardware, and this exhibit shows IBM's build of it.

## What you're looking at

An emulated RS/6000 40P, booting the genuine IBM firmware — the same ROM the real machine used — into AIX 4.3.3. The desktop is CDE, with its front panel across the bottom and four labelled workspaces.

The open window is **Netscape Communicator 4.08 for AIX**, reading IBM's own website as it existed in 1998, headlines and all. Nothing here reaches the modern internet: the machine sits on the gallery's private period network, which serves an archived corpus of the era's web to exhibits that are far too old to be exposed to anything else. The other software installed is of the same vintage — Ultimedia Services from the AIX 4.3.2 Bonus Pack driving a Crystal audio codec, and IBM's own PowerPC build of **Quake**, which the company distributed itself with id Software's permission.

Two honest notes. The exhibit runs AIX 4.3.3 rather than the 4.3.2 originally intended: 4.3.2's installer will not drive this machine's emulated SCSI controller, while 4.3.3 does, so the base is 4.3.3 with the 4.3.2 Bonus Pack layered on top. And the graphics adapter is a Matrox-based IBM GXT130P, because AIX will speak to no display hardware that any emulator previously offered — making this machine show a desktop at all meant writing that adapter in software first.

## Legacy

PReP is forgotten, and the open PowerPC desktop never arrived. The processor did far better than the platform: PowerPC ran Apple's Macintosh for a decade, then three consecutive generations of game console, and its descendants still ship as IBM POWER in machines that have nothing else in common with this one. AIX outlived nearly all its rivals — Solaris, IRIX, HP-UX, Tru64, several of them elsewhere on these walls — and is still supported today, more than thirty years after the first RS/6000.

What is on screen is the ordinary version of that history. Not the alliance or the specification, but a beige box under a desk in 1998, a Motif desktop in grey and orange, and a browser reading the day's news off a corporate homepage.
