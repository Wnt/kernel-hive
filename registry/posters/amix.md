## Origins

In 1990 Commodore did something almost nobody expected of the company that made the Amiga 500: it shipped a Unix workstation. The Amiga 3000UX paired the A3000's 68030 and MMU with **Amiga UNIX** — a genuine, licensed AT&T System V Release 4 port, done in-house with help from Lachman Associates, complete with the SVR4 filesystem switch, streams networking and a Berkeley compatibility package.

The choice of desktop is the part that still surprises people. Every other commercial Unix vendor of the era was converging on Motif: HP-UX had VUE, and CDE would soon standardise the look across HP, IBM, Sun and DEC. Commodore instead shipped **OPEN LOOK**, the Sun/AT&T design with its pushpins, rounded buttons and menu-button-drag idiom. AMIX is one of very few systems where OPEN LOOK was the *default* face of the machine.

Commodore even courted the workstation market properly for a moment — the A3000UX was demonstrated to Sun, and there was serious talk of Sun badging it as a low-end SPARC alternative. Nothing came of it.

## Significance

Amiga UNIX sold in tiny numbers. It cost far more than an Amiga, it wanted 8–16 MB of RAM and a large SCSI disk when both were expensive, and it competed against Sun and SGI machines built for the job. Version 2.1, from 1992, was effectively the last: Commodore's Unix effort ended well before the company itself did in 1994.

That scarcity is exactly why it belongs in a museum. Most people who owned an Amiga never saw one boot to a `login:` prompt, and the machine's reputation — games, video toasters, demoscene — has almost nothing to do with what is on this screen. AMIX is the Amiga's road not taken: the same custom chipset silicon underneath a multi-user, memory-protected, virtual-memory Unix from the same year Linux reached version 0.95.

## What you're looking at

An emulated **Amiga 3000** — Motorola 68030 with its MMU, 68882 FPU, 16 MB of RAM, Kickstart 2.04 — booting AMIX 2.1 from a SCSI disk to the OPEN LOOK Window Manager. The terminal reports the system's own idea of itself: `UNIX_System_V amix 4.0 2.1 Amiga (Unlimited) m68k`. Beside it are two of the era's standard fittings, the OPEN LOOK Calculator and an analogue `xclock`.

The screen is **monochrome, 640×512**, and that is authentic rather than a shortcut. AMIX's X server for the Amiga's own chipset is one bit deep; colour X on Amiga UNIX required a separate graphics board — Commodore's A2410, a TMS34010 card sold as an option — and hardly anyone had one. What most A3000UX users actually looked at is what is on this screen.

## Legacy

Amiga UNIX left no descendants, but it left a proof: the Amiga architecture could carry a serious operating system, and the hardware's reputation as a games machine was a market outcome, not a technical ceiling. The community that preserved it has been unusually diligent — the installation tape, the boot floppies and the patch disks all survive, which is why this exhibit could be installed the way a 1992 owner installed it, from tape, rather than cloned from someone else's disk.
