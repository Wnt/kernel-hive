## Origins

The Amiga's story should have ended in 1994, when Commodore International collapsed and took the platform's owner down with it. It didn't. The user base — famously loyal, famously stubborn — kept buying hardware, writing software and running bulletin boards through five years of ownership limbo, while the rights passed from Commodore's liquidators to Escom to Gateway. In 1999, Gateway-era Amiga Inc licensed the German developer Haage & Partner to do what no corporate owner had managed since Workbench 3.1 shipped with the last Commodore machines: release a new operating system for the classic 68k Amigas.

AmigaOS 3.5 was that release. It required a 68020 or better, Kickstart 3.1 and a hard disk — pushing the platform's big-box machines, the A1200s, A3000s and A4000s, rather than the bare A500s of the 80s. On top of the familiar Workbench it layered GlowIcons (Matt Chaput's pastel, pseudo-3D icon set that finally retired the four-colour look), ReAction GUI classes, native CD-ROM support with CacheCDFS, an HTML manual, and — the headline for 1999 — a bundled internet suite: a TCP/IP dialer, the AmigaMail client, and the AWeb II web browser.

## Significance

AmigaOS 3.5 is the swan song of the classic 68k line: the last AmigaOS of the 1990s, shipped by fans-turned-owners for a machine whose maker had been dead for half a decade. It proved the platform could still evolve — 3.9 followed in 2000, and the lineage continues today in OS 4 on PowerPC and in community releases — but 3.5 is the moment the community itself took the wheel.

AWeb II, the bundled browser, is its own piece of Amiga history. Written by Yvon Rozijn as one of the "big three" Amiga browsers (against IBrowse and Voyager), it was fast, HTML 3.2-capable with JavaScript support, and scriptable through ARexx like every proper Amiga application. Its inclusion on the OS 3.5 CD meant a 1999 Amiga owner unboxed a complete web-ready system — on an architecture whose first machine had shipped in 1985 with 256 KB of RAM.

## What you're looking at

An emulated Amiga 4000 — Motorola 68040, AGA chipset, Kickstart 3.1 — booting AmigaOS 3.5 from its hard disk to a 64-colour, 640×512 interlaced Workbench. The GlowIcons on the desktop are the release's signature; the spider-web icon is AWeb II, installed by the OS 3.5 CD's own "Install Internet Software" option. Open it and browse: the machine is wired to the museum's retronet, the lab's corpus-only recreation of the 1990s web, so the sites it reaches are period pages served the way a 1999 modem user would have seen them.

## Legacy

The classic Amiga never regained a mass market, but it never quite died either. AmigaOS 3.5's direct descendants are still maintained — Hyperion's OS 3.1.4 and 3.2 line ships new releases for 68k hardware in the 2020s — and AWeb went open source in 2002. The machines themselves live on under emulation: this exhibit runs on FS-UAE, one of the family of emulators descended from UAE, the "Unusable Amiga Emulator" of 1995 whose name became ironic almost immediately.
