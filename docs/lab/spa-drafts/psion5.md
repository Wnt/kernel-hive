## Origins

Psion had been building handheld computers long before smartphones gave the category a permanent name. Its Series 3 organizers of the early 1990s were successful precisely because they behaved like tiny computers rather than electronic notebooks: they had applications, storage, a keyboard and an operating system built for battery-powered use.

The **Series 5**, launched in 1997, was the next step. It introduced **EPOC32**, a new 32-bit operating system written in C++ and designed for ARM processors, with preemptive multitasking, graphical applications, pen input and communications built into the platform.

The hardware made the idea visible. Close it and it was a thick pocket organizer. Open it and the keyboard unfolded into a surprisingly usable miniature workstation beneath a wide touch-sensitive screen.

## Significance

EPOC32 is important because it is not merely a dead PDA operating system. It is the direct ancestor of **Symbian OS**.

The design had to solve problems desktop systems could mostly ignore: instant availability, tiny amounts of memory, aggressive power management, removable storage, unreliable mobile links, and applications that might remain resident for days. Those assumptions shaped the APIs and architecture that later ran millions of Nokia, Ericsson and other smartphones.

The Series 5 also represents a branch of mobile computing that did not survive. Modern phones abandoned hardware keyboards almost completely. Psion instead treated typing as a first-class activity and built a handheld that could genuinely be used to write.

## What you're looking at

The target exhibit is a **Series 5 or Series 5mx** running EPOC32, resting at its native system screen with the built-in productivity applications available. Agenda, Contacts, Word and Sheet are better demonstrations than a generic launcher because they show why someone carried this machine.

The browser pointer stands in for the stylus while the physical keyboard is represented by normal key input. The combination matters: EPOC was designed for both, not for one input method pretending to be the other.

## Legacy

In 1998 Psion's software business became part of **Symbian**, joined by major phone manufacturers. EPOC evolved into Symbian OS, and the assumptions developed for Psion handhelds moved into early smartphones.

Placed beside the planned Symbian S60 exhibit, the lineage becomes visible: the pocket word processor turns into the smartphone platform.
