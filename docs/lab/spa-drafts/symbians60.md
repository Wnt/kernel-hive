## Origins

Symbian OS grew directly from Psion's **EPOC32**. In 1998 Psion's software operation became Symbian Ltd, backed by phone manufacturers that wanted a shared smartphone platform rather than a desktop operating system squeezed into a handset.

Nokia built **Series 60**, later renamed **S60**, on top of Symbian. The first commercial Series 60 phone, the Nokia 7650, arrived in 2002 with a color screen, camera, installable applications and the now-familiar combination of status indicators, soft-key labels and a hierarchical app menu.

For much of the following decade, this was what a smartphone looked like to millions of people.

## Significance

S60 belongs in the museum because the pre-iPhone smartphone era is easy to compress into a footnote. It was not a footnote. Symbian became a dominant smartphone platform while phones were still primarily operated with a numeric keypad, directional pad and two context-sensitive soft keys.

The interface reflects that hardware. Every screen has to explain what the left and right soft keys mean *now*. Lists matter more than large touch targets. Applications coexist with telephony rather than replacing it.

Underneath, Symbian was a real multitasking operating system with a large native software ecosystem. Messaging clients, browsers, games, office viewers, navigation software and utilities turned phones into programmable computers before the modern app-store model standardized how that software was found.

## What you're looking at

The intended first exhibit is a well-supported **S60 3rd Edition** Nokia device under EKA2L1. A Nokia 5320-class target is a practical starting point: recognizable S60, mature enough to have a rich application environment, but still firmly from the keypad-and-soft-key era.

The best resting scene is the standby screen or application menu, with Contacts, Messaging and another period application one or two keypresses away. The point is to use the phone as a phone-shaped computer.

## Legacy

Symbian's market position collapsed rapidly after capacitive touchscreens, iOS and Android changed what users expected from a smartphone. Nokia's later transition through MeeGo and Windows Phone made the break even sharper.

But the mobile lineage does not disappear. Put Psion/EPOC on one side and Sailfish/Android on the other, and S60 becomes the missing middle: the moment a pocket organizer had already become a mass-market computer, before the interface model was rewritten.
