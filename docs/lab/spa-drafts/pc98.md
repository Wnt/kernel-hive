## Origins

NEC announced the **PC-9801** in October 1982. It was a 16-bit business computer built around an 8086-compatible processor and NEC's own graphics hardware, designed from the beginning to handle Japanese text and business applications efficiently.

That requirement mattered. Japanese computing could not simply inherit every assumption of Western 8-bit text displays. The PC-9801 combined higher performance, kanji-capable software and graphics in a machine that continued NEC's existing PC-8001/8801 ecosystem rather than copying IBM's new PC.

The result became the foundation of the **PC-9800 series**, one of the defining personal-computer platforms of Japan through the 1980s and well into the 1990s.

## Significance

The PC-98 is valuable precisely because it disrupts a familiar story.

From an American or European perspective, the 1980s PC market can look like a straight line from IBM compatibility to DOS and then Windows. Japan had a large parallel ecosystem whose machines ran similar categories of software but were not IBM-compatible at the hardware level.

Business applications, games, development tools and Japanese versions of DOS and Windows all grew around the platform. Software houses, peripheral makers, publishers and retailers formed what Japan's Information Processing Society describes as a whole “PC-98 culture.”

The exhibit therefore shows that “the PC” was not one universal machine even after the IBM PC had become dominant elsewhere.

## What you're looking at

The target exhibit is a later PC-9801 or PC-9821-class system running **Japanese MS-DOS 6.2 and/or Windows 3.1**. Windows is particularly effective museum material because it looks superficially familiar while the machine underneath is different.

## Legacy

IBM-compatible PCs eventually displaced the PC-98 as Windows standardized more of the Japanese PC market. NEC continued the line for years, but the special platform became less necessary as commodity PCs learned to do the work that had once required regional hardware.

The software and game culture built around it remains distinctive enough that PC-98 emulation has its own active preservation community.
